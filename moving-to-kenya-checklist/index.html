Moving to Kenya Checklist

Moving countries is not just about packing boxes; it is a sequence of legal, financial and logistical steps. Here is the exact order to do things so nothing important is left until it becomes urgent.

Start the checklist Read the FAQs

The short answer

The most common mistake people make is booking flights and shipping containers before securing their legal right to stay. Your visa or permit dictates your timeline. Everything else — housing, banking, schools, shipping — flows from that. This checklist is designed to put the administrative tasks in the right order, so you unlock the next step without delays.

1. Phase 1: Before You Book the Flight

This is the research and legal phase. Rushing this stage is the most expensive mistake you can make. If you are coming from the UK, USA, Canada, Italy or anywhere else, the rules are the same: paperwork takes time.

Determine your route

  • Are you visiting or moving? A visitor visa does not allow you to work or live here long-term. If you plan to work, start a business, or retire, you need the correct permit category.
  • Start the application early. Permit processing takes weeks or months. Do not book permanent shipping until you have an approval in principle or the permit in hand.

Document preparation

  • Passport validity: Ensure you have at least 6 months validity remaining.
  • Police Clearance (Certificate of Good Conduct): Required for most permits. This can take weeks to get from your home country.
  • Medical Report: A doctor must often confirm you are free from certain communicable diseases.
  • Legalisation: Any foreign marriage, birth, or police certificates must be legalised (often via an apostille) and translated into English if necessary. This is the slowest step in the entire process.

Temporary accommodation

Book at least two to four weeks in a serviced apartment or hotel. Do not sign a 12-month lease on a house you have never seen. You need time on the ground to decide which neighbourhood suits your commute, lifestyle, and family needs.

2. Phase 2: The First 14 Days

You have landed. The goal now is to establish your digital and financial footprint so you can function day-to-day.

Get a local SIM card

Head to a Safaricom shop (the dominant provider). You will need your passport to register the SIM. A local number is the gateway to everything else.

Set up M-Pesa

M-Pesa is mobile money, and Kenya runs on it. You will use it for groceries, transport, utilities, and almost everything else. Set it up immediately after getting your Safaricom line.

Apply for your KRA PIN (Tax PIN)

You need a Kenya Revenue Authority (KRA) PIN to open a bank account, buy property, or start a business. You can apply for this online, but if you are not a citizen, you will likely need to visit a KRA office in person for biometrics.

Open a bank account

Once you have your passport, KRA PIN, and proof of address (which can often be a letter from your temporary accommodation or employer), you can visit a bank to open an account. Requirements vary between banks like Equity, KCB, or Standard Chartered, so call ahead.

3. Phase 3: The First Month

Now it is time to put down roots. This phase is about turning your temporary stay into a settled life.

Secure long-term housing

Use your first two weeks to explore neighbourhoods. When you find a place:

  • Read the lease carefully (notice periods, deposit rules).
  • Ensure the landlord or agent provides a written inventory of the property's condition.
  • Set up utilities (water, electricity, internet). Note that many buildings require a backup water tank or generator.

Health insurance and hospitals

Private healthcare in Kenya is excellent but requires insurance. Arrange your health cover early. Identify your nearest hospital and clinic, and verify they accept your specific insurance policy.

School admissions (if moving with family)

If you are moving with children, school places are the single biggest factor in choosing a neighbourhood. Confirm the curriculum (British, American, IB, or Kenyan) and secure a place before committing to a lease nearby.

4. Phase 4: Settling In (Months 2-6)

Transport and driving

Depending on your area, you may need a car. Foreign driving licences are usually valid for a short period (often 3 months), after which you must apply for a Kenyan licence. Alternatively, many residents use ride-hailing apps (Uber, Bolt, Little) or hire a driver.

Build your local network

Settling in is as much social as it is administrative. Join local clubs, gyms, or professional networks. The expatriate community in Nairobi and the coast is welcoming and is often the best source of practical, on-the-ground advice.

Key points

  • Legal status first: Never book permanent shipping or resign from a job until your permit is approved.
  • Mobile money is king: M-Pesa is essential for daily life, groceries, and transport.
  • Tax PIN: You cannot open a bank account or start a business without a KRA PIN.
  • Rent before you buy: Do not commit to buying a house until you have lived in the area.
  • Legalise documents early: Apostilles and translations are the slowest part of the move.

6. Frequently asked questions

What is the very first thing I should do when planning a move to Kenya?

Determine your visa or permit route. You cannot plan your timeline, housing, or shipping without knowing how long you are legally allowed to stay. Apply for your entry authorisation or appropriate permit before booking flights.

What documents should I bring in my hand luggage?

Always carry your passport, printed entry authorisation or visa, marriage/birth certificates (if applying for dependant passes), and certified copies of any police clearances or medical reports in your hand luggage. Never put these in a shipping container.

Do I need a Kenyan Tax PIN to move there?

Yes. A KRA (Kenya Revenue Authority) PIN is required for almost all formal processes, including opening a bank account, registering a company, buying property, and often for employment. You should apply for this in your first week.

Can I open a bank account before I get my permit?

Requirements vary by bank. Many will allow you to open an account with a visitor visa if you have a valid passport, KRA PIN, proof of address, and a referral. However, some banks will insist on a valid residence or work permit. It is best to check with specific banks upon arrival.

What is M-Pesa and do I need it?

M-Pesa is Kenya's dominant mobile money system. It is not just for sending money to friends; you use it to pay for groceries, utilities, taxis, and even school fees. Setting it up with a local Safaricom SIM card is essential for daily life.

Should I ship my furniture or buy locally?

It depends on your budget and how long you plan to stay. Shipping a container takes months and involves customs clearance. Kenya has a vibrant furniture market, from local artisans to high-end imports, so many people choose to rent furnished accommodation first and buy locally once they know their neighbourhood.

How do I find a reputable school for my children?

Start early. Popular international schools have waiting lists. Request prospectuses, check which curriculum (British, American, IB, or Kenyan) suits your child's needs, and arrange to visit the campus in person before committing to fees.

Can I bring my pets to Kenya?

Yes, but it requires planning. You will need an import permit, a valid rabies vaccination certificate, a veterinary health certificate, and usually a pre-shipment blood test. It is highly recommended to use a specialised pet relocation agency to handle the paperwork.

What is the deal with security in Kenya?

Security varies significantly by neighbourhood. Most expatriates and middle-class Kenyans live in gated communities or apartment blocks with 24/7 guards. It is standard practice to have a secure compound, and we can help you identify areas that match your comfort level.

Do you guarantee that my move will go smoothly?

No one can guarantee a completely smooth international move, but we can help you avoid the common pitfalls. By planning the sequence correctly, ensuring your paperwork is legally sound, and working with trusted local partners, we minimise the risk of delays or stress.
